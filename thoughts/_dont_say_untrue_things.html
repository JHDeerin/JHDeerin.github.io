Don't Say Things That Aren't True

Ideally we would all say the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth. Alas, I am stupid, and sometimes don't know what the truth is.

I'm not talking about lying here (although yes, please don't lie). Instead, I'm talking about something less evil that wastes my time as a developer: people unintentionally saying things that are not true. Being overconfident. Jumping to conclusions. Things like:

  • "The load balancer's having errors, nothing's reaching our server" - when actually this person was checking the wrong application's logs, and it was our server's fault after all
  • A manager telling the sales team that yes, that feature was completed last year, you can promise that customer a demo - when the feature was absolutely not finished yet, and had only been discussed with a few mock-up screenshots
  • "Hey, your app is broken, I'm seeing a ton of errors, we need to talk and fix this" - and then when we get on the call, it turns out the customer was trying to run this web app on a machine not connected to the internet
Vague messages are their own can of worms, and can be equally frustrating - those "hey, your thing isn't working, please fix it" support tickets with no other information. But in those cases, at least I know we don't know anything, and that I'll need to do some detective work. But in the false information case, I am going to assume someone has already done their homework and skip those steps, and start working on something right away - the wrong thing. Detective work is often painfully slow, but work on the wrong thing is always wasted.

How does this happen? Why do people tell these honest untruths? There are only two reasons:

  1. They don't know the truth themselves. They've somehow or other come to believe something that's wrong.
  2. They do know the truth in their own head, but don't communicate it correctly to others.
You could have all the correct facts, but fumble the pass and draw a wrong conclusion through an error in logic. Or you could apply beautiful logic to some incorrect TikTok facts in your head and thusly be wrong. You can know a true thing and justify why to yourself, but through sloppy wording give someone else the wrong idea - or you might personally know that something is merely likely, not certain, but then talk and act as if that possibility is the only one that matters, and so lead others to think that way, too.

It doesn't matter, really, in the end - the result is the same. Someone walks away believing something false, and they're going to go out into the world and do damage. Hopefully not too much - just a little surprise when they learn the truth, no harm, no foul - but in the worst case, years of work, billions of dollars, and possibly human lives will be wasted on preventable mistakes. Most of us won't be making screw-ups that gargantuan in our lives, but we can certainly give our IT friends the occasional sleepless night, or send our family driving an hour to the wrong address (who'd have thought "Wiltshire Drive" and "Wiltshire Way" were in the same town?).

Ideally, you should tell the truth. But if you can't do that, at least do this: don't say things that are not true. It's okay to say "I don't know" if you're honestly not sure.

The trouble is, of course, these are unintentional falsehoods - by definition they're hard to notice, "unknown unknowns" and "you don't know what you don't know"s. How can you possibly avoid them? You can't - not completely. You will make mistakes, have misunderstandings, simply know things that just ain't so. But I don't think it's entirely hopeless; I think you can improve and get better over time at throwing less untruths into the void. Here are some things I do, personally, that I think are helpful:

  1. Before you say anything, ask yourself: "Is this true? Do I KNOW this is true? How do I know it's true?" This is basic, but a double-check will save you from being overconfident and jumping to conclusions, stating things like "I know it's team X's microservice, that's the 3rd time it's down this week" when actually all you've seen is one 500 HTTP error code that could have a dozen different causes.
    • This level of paranoia can get exhausting, so do basic triage here - the more important and permanent what you're saying is, the more you should ask this question. Hold lunch banter over football to a different standard than financial reports to your CEO.
  2. Share what you see, not just what you think. Instead of saying "Team X's microservice is down again", say "Team X's microservice is down again - I'm seeing a 500 error in /somelink/logs." This is way better to me; by sharing more of the story, even with a false conclusion, you let people know why you think something is happening, and that gives them a chance to sanity-check what you're saying. Just last week, a coworker caught that I'd misdiagnosed a demo failure because I posted the exact query I ran, and she saw I'd typed the wrong customer ID. Separate facts from conclusions (knowing that sometimes you can get both wrong).
    • There is a danger here of getting too wordy with details, and overwhelming people; when in doubt, just add 1 sentence explaining why you think something is true ("I think X, because Y").
  3. Practice being careful with the truth in everyday life; make a habit of it. Easy to say, hard to do. But it's easy to get sloppy with details if you have a habit of "not letting the truth get in the way of a good story."
  4. Don't be afraid to say "I don't know." Or better yet, "I don't know; give me a minute to check." Or "I'm not sure, but my best guess is…" - if you're not sure, let people know that somehow. This can be frustrating and embarrassing in the moment, but ultimately it's better than leading someone astray, and long-term builds trust that you're not careless.
